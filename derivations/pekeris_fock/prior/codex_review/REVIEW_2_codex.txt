Review of REQUEST_2_numerics.txt — 2026-10-19

Verdict

The main useful result is the switch from individual exponential columns to
an asymptotic expansion of the selected slow-subspace boundary map. The
Riccati recurrence and unsigned-Stirling conversion are correct. The
numerics provide a useful independent exterior construction at sufficiently
large R, and the matching experiment is credible for the reported E and a21.

However, “diverges at R=1,” “d=6 needs R>=8–10,” and the attribution to poles
at large imaginary height are not established convergence statements. In
particular the d=2 pole that was found cannot explain divergence at R=1.
The d=6 matching numbers also greatly overstate the accuracy of the WHOLE
boundary map if read that way. There is no demonstrated improvement in
speed, error control, or full-helium accuracy over panels.

I read the supplied source, logs, CSV data, figure, the underlying
cusp_matching.jl/probe.jl, and REVIEW_1. I made no changes in
../exterior_factorial. Local diagnostic scripts and logs are:
 audit_checks.jl / audit_checks.log
 audit_poles.jl / audit_poles.log
 audit_matching_d2.log
The scripts use the same model matrices, so these checks are independent
of the summation/continuation in relevant places, not independent checks
of the angular model or its physical boundary selection. This is a
numerical and mathematical audit, not a certified convergence proof.

1. Algebra: the boundary-map expansion is the right improvement

For Y=Phi' Phi^{-1}, substitution gives
 Y'+Y^2+(5/R+2U)Y-Lambda/R^2+D/R+T=0.
Writing L(H)=(Y0+2U)H+H Y0, the coefficient equations in exterior_lib.jl
are correct, including the separate n=1 equation and the +(Lambda) at n=2.
The vectorization/Kronecker Sylvester operator has the correct ordering.
Newton refinement of the slow solvent is also consistent with this L.

There is a concrete reason the small slow-slow gaps disappear. With
A0=[0 I; -T -2U], use coordinates (Phi, Phi'-Y0 Phi). The constant system
is block triangular with diagonal blocks Y0 and -(2U+Y0). Thus the latter
has the fast eigenvalues, whereas Y0 has the slow eigenvalues. Eigenvalues
of L are lambda_slow-lambda_fast. These, rather than internal slow-root
differences, are the primary candidate Borel singularities for the map.
For the tested real spectra the candidates are negative. Nonlinear Borel
convolutions can generate additive combinations of these locations; the
list of singularities is not necessarily JUST the pairwise gaps.

The measured gaps 3.5560, 3.1121, 2.8028 are sensible primary analyticity
scales. Calling them exact Borel radii still requires a nonzero singularity
at the nearest candidate. An invertible Sylvester operator plus coefficient
ratios is evidence, not that proof. The gain comes from representing a
subspace invariant under changes of column basis, eliminating internal
slow-mode connection ambiguities.

2. Stirling conversion and omega: no algebraic bug found

With the stated Laplace convention,
 Y-Y0 = integral exp(-Rt) yhat(t) dt,
 yhat(t)=sum_j Y_{j+1} t^j/j!,
set t=-log(1-u)/omega. Then
 Y-Y0 = (1/omega) integral_0^1
          (1-u)^(R/omega-1) yhat(-log(1-u)/omega) du.
Using L(u)^j/j!=sum_k |s(k,j)| u^k/k! gives exactly
 B_k=sum_{j=0}^k |s(k,j)| omega^(-j-1) Y_{j+1},
 Y=Y0+sum_k B_k/(R/omega)_{k+1}.
The beta integral cancels the k!, so its absence in B_k is correct. The
indexing in factorial_coeffs and the Pochhammer updates are correct.

A negative Borel singularity t=-g maps to u=1-exp(omega*g). Keeping it
outside |u|<1 requires omega*g>log(2). The implemented choice
omega=1.15 log(2)/g satisfies this, even after the displayed rounding.
The nearest candidate maps to about -1.219. Thus omega is not accidentally
inverted. It is a heuristic choice, not an optimum or a convergence
certificate: transformed-Borel growth at u=1 and all other singularities
must also be controlled. Larger omega changes both the singularity geometry
and the algebraic convergence rate; one should compare fixed-accuracy cost,
not assume that a larger scale is uniformly better.

There is substantial cancellation in the coefficient conversion. Running
at high precision is necessary, and production calculations should retain
precision until the final matching calculation. factorial_partial_sums,
factorial_Y, and taylor_connect return Float64 matrices. In particular the
high-precision factorial value is rounded BEFORE the long Taylor step.
This prevents an arbitrary-precision accuracy claim for the complete path.
It is consistent with the reported double-precision goals.

3. Riccati poles: real, but the claimed convergence explanation is wrong

The first-order linear system has rational coefficients singular only at
R=0 in the finite plane. Its continued solution frame has no finite
singularity away from zero on a chosen branch. A zero of det(Phi), however,
can create a pole in Y=Phi' Phi^{-1}; it is a coordinate singularity of the
subspace, not a singularity of the full solution frame.

I independently checked the three scan candidates by integrating only to
a safe complex center, expanding the LINEAR fundamental matrix there, and
finding its singularity as a zero of that matrix determinant (via the
smallest singular vectors). Halving the RK4 step from .004 to .002 gave:
 d=2: R approximately 0.637277253968 + 1.850962298017 i
 d=4: R approximately 1.405225189970 + 4.163131106851 i
 d=6: R approximately 0.55950387010  + 3.47330214316  i
Conjugates follow from real coefficients. These are stable approximate
zeros, not validated enclosures or an exhaustive enumeration of poles.
The original coarse Riccati scan therefore found genuine nearby features,
but max-norm peaks/blow-up alone would not have certified poles.

The distinction between Re(p) and Im(p) is essential. An exact scalar
example in THIS factorial basis is
 1/(R-p) = sum_{k>=0} (p/omega)_k /
                        [omega (R/omega)_{k+1}].
For a generic nonterminating case, its kth term is proportional to
 k^((p-R)/omega-1), and the remainder after K terms is proportional to
 K^((p-R)/omega). This expansion converges for Re(R)>Re(p), although the
function is regular at many other points outside that half-plane.
The imaginary part creates logarithmic oscillations and can produce
very large prefactors through Gamma(p/omega). It does not by itself set
an abscissa equal to |p|, or to |Im(p)|.

Consequences:
 * The displayed d=2 pole has Re(p)=.6373<1. It cannot explain divergence
   at R=1. It predicts potentially slow, oscillatory algebraic convergence
   from its contribution, including at R=.75, not fast convergence.
 * The d=4 pole has Re(p)=1.4052>1. An actual uncancelled pole there rules
   out a single convergent factorial representation of Y on a right
   half-plane extending past that pole to R=1. This IS a genuine obstruction
   to this particular chart/basis, despite regularity along the real axis.
 * The displayed d=6 pole has Re(p)=.5595<1. Its imaginary height can cause
   difficult transients, but it does not explain an abscissa near 8–10.

My d=2 rerun used coefficients through order 802 at 3072-bit precision:
 omega=.224, R=1: errors at K=100,300,400,600,800 were
   3.3726, .72507, .72180, .35724, .27265.
 omega=.224, R=2: errors at K=100,300,800 were
   7.066e-6, 1.898e-8, 6.769e-11.
At R=.75 the errors oscillate around O(100) through K=800. Changing omega
to .3 also gives a slowly decreasing, oscillatory R=1 sequence overall.
These tests do not prove convergence, but emphatically do not establish
the advertised d=2 divergence. The original log already showed a falling
R=1 error from K=50 to 300.

“d=6 needs R>=8–10” is defensible only as a practical radius for obtaining
near-machine accuracy with about 260 terms in these experiments. Even the
supplied d=6 R=5 sequence decreases from 11 to .53 to .0022; calling it
divergent would be unwarranted. Poles above |Im R|=6 are a plausible source
of large transient constants, but their existence/location and relevance
were not tested by scan_poles.jl (which stops at 6). A convergence threshold
requires rightmost pole locations and/or transformed-Borel growth bounds,
not just imaginary heights. The finite-order data do not distinguish all
these possibilities.

4. Representations without these chart poles

Yes, one can remove the artificial finite-R singularities from the
REPRESENTATION of the subspace, though this does not automatically prove
that one factorial series converges down to .5.

Let X be the full 2m by m slow solution frame satisfying X'=A(R)X.
It retains rank m away from zero, since linear evolution is invertible.
It is holomorphic on a simply connected domain avoiding zero; globally
one must allow monodromy/logarithms, or work on the universal cover of C*.
It has no compulsory poles at zeros of its top m by m block. Its columns
still have internal normalization/connection issues at infinity, so
columnwise asymptotic summation is not the best way to exploit this fact.

The homogeneous Plucker vector
 Xi = X_1 wedge ... wedge X_m
represents the same Grassmannian point and satisfies the LINEAR compound
system Xi'=A^[m](R) Xi. Its coefficient singularities are again only at
zero. Individual minors can vanish, but all cannot vanish together.
Normalizing by a minor reintroduces poles; retaining homogeneous
coordinates avoids this. Factoring exp(R sum(lambda_slow)) and a nonzero
power gauge on a chosen branch does not introduce finite zeros/poles.
The constant compound eigenvalues are sums of m characteristic roots.
The selected all-slow sum is separated from other choices by replacements
of slow roots with fast roots, so the nearest candidate differences again
involve the slow-fast gap rather than the small slow-slow gaps.

This gives a concrete promising route to a pole-free homogeneous exterior
expansion. It is not yet a convergence theorem for this matrix model:
one must establish the normalized asymptotic solution, its Borel domain
and growth, and factorial tail bounds. Analyticity of a function on a slit
plane by itself does not establish convergence of a particular factorial
basis. There can also be poor conditioning when recovering Y from a small
chosen minor, even when Xi is regular.

The full compound system costs binomial(2m,m) coordinates: 70 for d=2,
48,620 for d=4, and 601,080,390 for d=6. Thus full Plucker evolution is a
useful small-model experiment, not an attractive naive d=6 algorithm.
More practical choices are an atlas of local graph charts, a transported
frame with QR/pivoting, or structured low-rank compound methods.

A constant slow-fast block transformation also helps: express the subspace
as a graph of fast coordinates over slow coordinates in an eigenbasis of
A0. Its leading graph is zero and the Sylvester gaps are explicit. But
this merely changes which projection can become singular; it does not
prove a globally nonsingular single graph. Likewise (cI-Y)^(-1) is the
chart Phi(c Phi-Phi')^(-1). It can remove some old poles and introduce
zeros of det(c Phi-Phi'). Failure for c=4,8,16 and two omega values is
not a no-go theorem for all charts or homogeneous representations.

For reaching R=.5 NOW, the existing linear-frame Taylor continuation is
already a sound route: from any positive R1 its guaranteed analytic disk
|R-R1|<R1 contains every 0<R<R1. It crosses Riccati chart poles without
expanding Y through them. Large order/precision and final inversion
conditioning must be controlled; several short panels usually cost less
than one long expansion. There is no need to prove a new global factorial
theorem merely to continue the selected finite-model subspace to .5.

5. Matching: the reported scalar agreement is credible, with a major caveat

The supplied matching logs do report |dE|=4.0–4.5e-12 and
|da21|=2.9–3.1e-14 for all three d values. Both solvers stop at mismatch
2e-11, so same-sign energy differences of that size do not measure the
factorial truncation error. They are consistent with different stopping
points. Re-solve both with tighter tolerances and vary K, Taylor order,
R1, precision, and rm before assigning extra digits.

The d=6 whole-map accuracy is much worse than those scalar differences.
I isolated the Taylor error by starting at R1=9 with the accurate PANEL
value, rather than a factorial approximation. At R0=1 I obtained:
 Taylor order      norm(Y_Taylor-Y_reference)
     320                 1.4304e-4
     420                 6.0230e-8
     460                 2.1062e-9
     520                 1.1325e-11
     600                 1.1840e-14
This reproduces the supplied taylor_rerun.log trend and shows that order
420 does not give a 1e-12 boundary map. The initial R1 error is not the
cause of this discrepancy.

Nevertheless, applying that matrix error to the normalized null vector
of the panel matching matrix gave only about 2e-16 at orders 320–600.
The relevant small matching eigenvalue also stayed within about 1e-15.
Thus the large map error lies almost entirely in directions irrelevant
to this matched state. This explains the apparently contradictory energy
agreement; it is not evidence that the Taylor or matching code is broken.
It also means E and a21 alone are weak tests of the full exterior map.
Report both the full-map norm and the state-specific action/residual.

The Taylor recurrence itself agrees with direct substitution into the
linear ODE and with the panel recurrence. Its guaranteed convergence disk
is determined by zero, rather than Riccati poles. The method is a high-order
ODE continuation, even when compressed into one Taylor expansion. It is
not simply “Fock plus one global factorial series.”

6. Finite-rmax boundary error: conclusion mostly right, statement too broad

At d=2, R=12, I reproduced an approximately 6e-17 difference between the
40-term asymptotic sum and the rmax=30/order=30 panel reference. The default
rmax=24 panels agree there to about 1e-16. This supports the claim that
the two-term endpoint initialization is not the accuracy bottleneck at
small matching radius in this test. Backward propagation suppresses
errors that change the slow/fast graph.

But the TWO-TERM boundary value itself is not that accurate. At d=2,
R=12, its difference from the high-order value is about .0725; at R=24
it is about .0177. Those are endpoint errors, subsequently damped inward.
At the endpoint R=24 the default cuspexterior literally returns that
two-term approximation. Do not extend the small-rm accuracy claim to all R.

Nor was the quoted 8e-17 result demonstrated for every d. In my check the
40-term R=12 asymptotic sum differs from the refined panels by about
4.6e-16 for d=4 but 3.4e-8 for d=6. The default and refined panels at R=12
for d=6 agree to about 1.6e-14. An asymptotic truncation must be selected
and checked separately at each d and R; a fixed order 40 is not a
uniform accuracy prescription. None of these comparisons is a rigorous
bound, although the agreement at rm=1 under parameter changes is strong
practical evidence.

7. Per-exponent test: useful evidence, with one endpoint-coefficient defect

The recurrence and solvability correction in per_exponent.jl agree with
REVIEW_1 after beta=-mu. The close-root numbers are good evidence for
actual slow-slow Borel obstructions in these examples. However:
 * .088, .032, .0019 are the smallest gaps across the selected columns,
   not the radius of every column (the log itself shows different radii).
 * A ratio over orders 100–120 is an estimate, not an exact radius theorem.
 * formal_solution(...,N) leaves h_N's free null component uncorrected.
   It fixes h_{n-1} at iteration n, so the last returned coefficient has
   only the chosen transverse component. The radius estimate compares
   finalized h_100 with unfinished h_120. Compute through N=121 before
   using h_120, or estimate entirely from completed interior coefficients.
   This can affect the quoted finite-order ratios even though it does
   not invalidate the evident small-gap scale.

For nonminimal slow columns there are also candidate POSITIVE-axis Borel
singularities from still slower columns. A direct real-axis Borel integral
needs prescriptions/cancellations; a small Taylor radius alone is not the
whole problem. “Per-column hopeless” is too strong as a mathematical
statement, but a direct common factorial construction of all columns is
clearly poorly suited to these spectra. The subspace formulation is a
substantive improvement.

8. Sensitivity and coefficient type: supports REVIEW_1 point 2, with its caveats

sensitivity.jl measures RELATIVE response: it divides perturbation norms
by norm(Phi) and 1e-12. The a perturbation has relative norm 1e-12 and the
E perturbation is relative in E. The generic recurrence is consistent
with cuspcoeff. High-precision evaluation separates this conditioning
experiment from ordinary floating-point cancellation.

The experiment strongly supports generic loss of tail accuracy from
uncertain origin data. It does not restore the original absolute
impossibility claim, and it does not show a universal exponent 2*kappa.
Exact data and arbitrary precision remain different from uncertain data;
special perturbations tangent to the selected solution remain exceptions.

The last-interval logarithmic slopes of the a amplification are about
3.95 (d=2) and 4.53 (d=4). The corresponding largest-fast minus largest-slow
gaps printed by this code are about 4.36 and 4.38, respectively. These
are broadly comparable finite-R rates, not numerical confirmation of
an exact equality. Powers of R, competing modes, perturbation projections,
and nonnormality matter over .5<=R<=6. Larger-R fits require more accurate
baseline E and amplitudes so that the denominator is still the desired
slow solution rather than its own growing contamination.

The “type” plots need more care. At n=170 the selected-a estimates are
4.325 (d=2) and 4.494 (d=4); the generic matrix estimates are 4.394 and
4.758, versus largest root moduli 4.712 and 4.858. These are compatible
with a fast spectral scale, but do not establish the limiting numbers.
The max over log powers k is not itself the growth type of the evaluated
sum of c_nk R^n(log R)^k: cross-k cancellation and continuation on different
logarithmic sheets matter. Moreover the supplied a and E are approximate,
so tiny unwanted fast components can control very-high-order behavior.
The generic norm also converts coefficients to Float64 before measuring
them, limiting meaningful extrapolation to much larger n.

Thus the sensitivity data confirm the practical conditioning mechanism
in REVIEW_1 point 2, not an inability of exact analytic continuation or
resummation to reach any prescribed finite R. Coefficient growth alone
would not prove that conditioning conclusion.

9. What is valuable versus panels, and what to do next

Valuable here:
 (a) A well-defined algebraic expansion of the invariant boundary map,
     free of the tiny internal slow gaps that obstruct columnwise work.
 (b) A usable large-R factorial initialization that avoids specifying a
     finite-rmax two-term boundary value, conditional on its convergence.
 (c) A cross-check of the panel exterior and a clear illustration that
     full-map and state-specific matching accuracies can differ enormously.
 (d) Concrete chart poles and a promising small-d homogeneous-coordinate
     experiment to distinguish chart limitations from intrinsic ones.

Not established: a global two-series closed form down to arbitrary R0>0,
a convergence certificate at .5, a computational advantage over panels,
or any improvement to the finite angular model's physical threshold.
The hybrid still performs ODE continuation and shares the same interior
recurrence, angular discretization, and slow-subspace selection as panels.
Its logged matching costs (7,57,330 seconds) are not a matched-accuracy
benchmark against panel costs.

My next experiment would be d=2 homogeneous Plucker coordinates, with the
minimal compound exponential removed: compare factorial partial sums near
R=.5,1 with the current Y chart, varying precision and omega. In parallel
at the mathematical level, determine the transformed-Borel boundary
growth; do not infer an abscissa from a few hundred terms. For a practical
solver I would keep short linear-frame panels/QR continuation, improve
the infinity initialization if needed, and report separate exterior-map,
state-residual, root-finder, and angular-truncation error budgets.
